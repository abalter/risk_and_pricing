**The first.** The kernel of $\Delta_i\Delta_j$ is exactly the additive functions: if $\Delta_i\Delta_j g \equiv 0$ then $g(v_i,v_j) = g(v_i,b_j) + g(b_i,v_j) - g(b_i,b_j)$ for all $v$, which is a function of $x_i$ plus a function of $x_j$. The statistics construction you're thinking of is the same expansion with a different kill operator. Write $E_j$ for "average over $x_j$." Then $g = \prod_j \big(E_j + (1 - E_j)\big)\, g$, and multiplying out gives one component per subset, with $1 - E_j$ playing the role of $\Delta_j$ and $E_j$ the role of $B_j$. Its interaction components are the pieces that averaging over any of their variables kills; ours are the pieces that setting any of their variables to base kills. The algebra is identical.

The difference is where the commuting comes from. The $B_j$ commute unconditionally, since setting two different variables to fixed values can be done in either order. The $E_j$ commute only when the averaging distribution is a product, which is the independence assumption you mentioned; under dependence, "average over $x_j$ holding the rest" is a conditional expectation and the order matters, and the decomposition into unique components breaks. So the independence assumption in "cancel interactions by averaging" is the price of choosing an average as the kill operator, and the point-anchored version never pays it. That is the paper's book-dependence theme in operator form.

**The second.** There are two candidate small parameters, and only one of them is useful for tables. The first is displacement. For small $v_i - b_i$, $\Delta_i \approx (v_i - b_i)\,\partial_i$, so $D \approx (v-b)\cdot\nabla$ and $e^{D}$ becomes $e^{(v-b)\cdot\nabla}$, the translation operator of Taylor's theorem. That is the propagator you're hearing: $e^{D}$ carries $g$ from the base cell to the target cell, and it does so exactly, at any finite displacement, because $\Delta_i^2 = 0$ truncates each $e^{\Delta_i}$ to $1 + \Delta_i$ where $e^{h\partial_i}$ never terminates. Truncating in displacement is just Taylor expansion about the base, fine near it and useless for a rating table, which has to be right at cells far from base.

The second small parameter is interaction order, and here the series terminates on its own. The $k$-th term of $\sum_k \frac{1}{k!}[D^k g]_b$ is the sum of all $k$-way tables, and if $g$ is built from pieces each involving at most $d$ variables, then $D^k g = 0$ for every $k > d$: a GLM with terms up to order $d$, a GAM with smooths of at most $d$ variables, boosted trees of depth $d$ or fit under interaction constraints. So in practice the order isn't a small parameter you hope for; it's a knob you set when fitting, and the series is then exact at that order with no truncation error. Depth-2 boosting, for instance, is exactly a base rate times one-way and two-way tables. Four things need care, though, and they're where the real content is.

First, the relativities have to be computed with every other variable held fixed. Second, there are two questions here and they have different tests. "Does any interaction among the variables in $S$ exist anywhere" is base-free: $\Delta_S g \not\equiv 0$ as a function of everything, base corners included. "Which tables are nonzero at my chosen base" is $\Delta_S g|_b \not\equiv 0$, and it is base-dependent. The base-free invariant is which subsets carry *some* joint dependence, and for term selection that's the question you want.

Third, dependence among the covariates leaves corners empty even in an infinite book. With finite data the cell means are noisy and the decomposition applied to raw data reads noise as high-order interaction; GLMs, GAMs and shrinkage are machinery for deciding which anchored tables are distinguishable from one. That's a sharper statement of the problem than "choose which terms to include," and it suggests the natural diagnostic, which is to compute the anchored tables from the fitted model and from the raw cross-classified cells where the data allow, and look at where they disagree.

**The derivation.**

*Step 1. The model.* $\ln\mu(\mathbf{x}) = f_0 + \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}} f_T(\mathbf{x}_T)$, terms indexed by their variable sets.

*Step 2. What we want.* Functions $g_S(\mathbf{x}_S)$, one for each nonempty $S \subseteq [p]$, such that

$$
\text{(a)}\quad \ln\mu(\mathbf{x}) = \ln\mu(\mathbf{b}) + \sum_{S\neq\emptyset} g_S(\mathbf{x}_S) \ \text{ for all } \mathbf{x}, \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\quad \mathbb{B}_i\, g_S = 0 \ \text{ for every } i \in S .
$$

(a) says the tables price the model; (b) says each table is separately interpretable.

*Step 3. Apply* $\mathbb{B}_{-S}\Delta_S$ to both sides of (a). On the $g$ side: the constant dies (rule 1); $g_T$ with $T \not\supseteq S$ dies (rule 1: some $\Delta_i$ finds no $x_i$); $g_T$ with $T \supsetneq S$ dies (rule 3: some $\mathbb{B}_j$, $j \in T \setminus S$, commutes through $\Delta_S$ and kills it); $g_S$ survives, and expanding $\Delta_S$ every term with a $\mathbb{B}_i$, $i \in S$, dies by rule 3, leaving $\mathbb{V}_S\, g_S = g_S(\mathbf{v}_S)$. On the $f$ side, the same rule 1 kills every $f_T$ with $T \not\supseteq S$, and for $T \supseteq S$ the operator $\mathbb{B}_{-S}$ has only the variables $T \setminus S$ to act on (rule 2). Equating:

$$
g_S(\mathbf{v}_S) \;=\; \sum_{T \supseteq S} \mathbb{B}_{T\setminus S}\,\Delta_S\, f_T .
$$

This is the recipe, and it is forced: any $g$'s satisfying (a) and (b) must be these. For each table $S$, take every term containing $S$, difference it in the variables of $S$, set its remaining variables to base, and add.
